# Speed control, not noise reduction, earns modeling next

No Panasonic document mentions noise reduction for the RR-DR60. The owner's manual, the service manual, the 1999–2000 US web catalog and the Japanese spec sheet are all silent on it, the earliest instance of the claim found is one unsourced 2020 article (S-004), and the schematic has neither a noise-reduction circuit nor a DSP control pin for one. So **stage 6 should stay unmodeled**, and A-009 should be rewritten from "may exist" to "no evidence; claim traced". **Stage 14 (the clock)** is a single 8 MHz ceramic resonator that times both processors. The codec runs at 6 kHz, exactly the MSM7702's 6.0 kHz minimum. A per-unit clock error of ±0.5–1 % produces **no pitch error at all** when a unit plays back its own recording, so the deviation parameter A-011 promises would buy nothing audible. The playback **SPEED CONTROL**, by contrast, is a real five-level firmware feature: S6 is wired to the microcontroller's IRQ3, and a 1999 Panasonic US page advertises "Slow/Fast Play (+/-40%)". The slow half of that range cannot be a codec clock change inside the datasheet's operating range, so for the slow steps the device either resamples in the DSP (pitch drops) or time-scales in the decoder (pitch is kept); a codec clock change remains possible for the fast steps only (a hybrid). One five-minute capture decides which. **Stage 15 (power)** turns out to be three firmware-switched rails plus an always-on boost converter. The speaker amplifier runs on the raw battery, so its clip ceiling falls by about **9 dB over a 10-hour alkaline discharge** (estimated from datasheet curves), far outside A-031's registered 1.0–1.4 V; that is audible only on material that reaches the ceiling. The record path runs on regulated rails and is largely immune to battery state. Whether a recording starts with a transient depends on an undocumented firmware choice: does the analog rail switch on at REC? The bounded range runs from inaudible to a full-scale thump that ducks the first 1.4 s through the limiter. The OKI MSM7702 datasheet (S-003) has now been read in full, and it moves three typical values, one passband limit and the transmit idle-noise figure that the registers quote. Recommended next: the speed control as the next spec, defining the control, the ratios and the API with the mechanism left open until a capture (the evidence request posted now); the battery-dependent speaker ceiling as an off-by-default option; start-of-item transients only after a capture; and a documentation chore for about twenty register corrections.

Every claim below comes from the eight research notes in [`research_notes/Noise reduction clock and power/`](<../research_notes/Noise reduction clock and power/>). Each note is cited inline where its finding is used, alongside the primary URLs the notes give. "Computed" and "estimated" mark figures derived from printed component values or datasheet curves. None of them comes from a real unit; the project still has no unit and no reference recording.

## Four Panasonic documents name VAS and speed but never noise reduction

The documentary record is unusually complete for a 1998 consumer product, and it is consistently silent. The US owner's manual (RQT4606-1P, S-001) uses the word "noise" exactly once, in a troubleshooting line about a dirty earphone plug. The signal features it names are microphone sensitivity (five levels) and VAS ([manual OCR](https://archive.org/stream/Panasonic_RR-DR60_Microcassette_Recorder_User_Manual/Panasonic_RR-DR60_Microcassette_Recorder_User_Manual_djvu.txt)). The Panasonic US product page, archived in identical form in 1999 and 2000, lists CELP coding, the 60-minute memory, Intro-Scan, protect marking, an automatic time stamp, the electret microphone, Digital VAS and "Slow/Fast Play (+/-40%)". It lists **no noise reduction, filter or AGC** ([Wayback 1999-09-13](https://web.archive.org/web/19990913100520/http://www.prodcat.panasonic.com:80/shop/product.asp?sku=RR-DR60)). The Japanese spec sheet (仕様書) names fifteen parts, including a 可変速再生つまみ (variable-speed playback knob). Nowhere on it do ノイズリダクション, ノイズキャンセル, ノイズカット, 雑音 or ローカット appear ([Panasonic partner PDF](https://partner.connect.panasonic.com/sites/default/files/CS/PCO/Product%20Documents/Basic%20Docs/asset/solweb/first/shnm_files/sks-v/RR-DR60.PDF)). The service manual (S-002) specifies "CELP", "6 kHz", "4 kbit/s" and "8 bit µ-law", and nothing about noise reduction ([Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/manual_RRDR60_SM_PANASONIC_EN)). A marketing page that bothers to list "Protect Marking" would very likely have listed a noise feature if Panasonic had considered it one. The sibling RR-QR series pages and the RR-QR240 spec sheet list none either. Panasonic's first IC-recorder "noise cancel" found is a playback feature on the RR-US310 in 2013 ([AV Watch](https://av.watch.impress.co.jp/docs/news/584343.html)), and the class's first such claim found is Olympus's DS-330 in March 2002 ([MacTech](https://www.mactech.com/2002/03/08/olympus-introduces-usb-digital-voice-recorder/)) ([nr_documentary_evidence.md](<../research_notes/Noise reduction clock and power/nr_documentary_evidence.md>)). That is strong evidence that the feature was never documented. It does not prove the firmware lacks every kind of noise processing.

The claim has a datable origin. The earliest instance found that attributes "onboard noise reduction and auto-gain functions" to the RR-DR60 is Higgypop's article, published 2020-02-13 and already carrying the sentence in the first Wayback capture of 2020-02-18 ([Wayback](https://web.archive.org/web/20200218211242/https://www.higgypop.com/news/panasonic-rr-dr-60/)). It cites no source, and the same article contains clear errors ("sample the sound six times per second"; an "18kHz" upper limit). Before 2020 the community said roughly the opposite: a 2015 comment says investigators like the DR60 for its "**lack of noise filters**". The often-quoted 2014 comment about "auto-gain, noise reduction (yuck)", read in full, describes **a different, unnamed recorder** ([Paranormal New England](https://paranormalnewengland796925647.wordpress.com/2014/12/18/panasonic-rr-dr60-ic-recorder-evp-legend-longer/)). Later pages copy Higgypop verbatim ([huntdogman](https://huntdogman.com/panasonic-rr-dr60-a-legendary-evp-recorder/)). Higgypop's "auto-gain" half has since gained independent support, since S-002 shows the limiter registered as A-028. Its "noise reduction" half has none. Its own description ("remove hiss and low-end rumble") matches what the codec's 225–2550 Hz transmit band-pass (A-027) and the VAS gate already do. The inference here, about wording rather than a source: **band-limiting plus VAS gating, with the decoder's postfilter (A-041), is the most likely documented behavior a listener would call "noise reduction"**. The same documents also date the product. The manual's print code "F0698" (June 1998 by Panasonic's F + MMYY pattern), the service manual's © 1998 and the US catalog's 1999–2001 lifetime make the **earliest dated document June 1998 (the US manual)**. Nothing supports the "mid-1990s" that S-004 and this project's mission statement repeat, though an earlier (1997) Japanese launch is not excluded.

The hardware leaves only one place a noise suppressor could live. S-002 shows no analog noise-reduction circuit. No DSP pin is named for one either: /DTMFCS is tied high, and LMUTE, PSACK, PLLSLEEP and GO5–GO10 are unconnected ([service_manual_power_clock.md](<../research_notes/Noise reduction clock and power/service_manual_power_clock.md>)). Any such function would be firmware on the MN1931712BB, and that firmware is undocumented. The 1997 Panasonic Semiconductor Selection Guide documents the MN1930-series 3 V, 16-bit DSPs (MN1932801, MN1933211): 27 / 21.7 ns instruction cycles, a double-speed MAC, bit-reverse addressing, max/min instructions and 6K words of data RAM. Its digital-phone reference designs use them as "Speech Signal Processors" ([bitsavers PDF](http://www.bitsavers.org/components/panasonic/1997_Panasonic_Semiconductor_Selection_Guide.pdf)). By part numbering only (an inference), the MN1931712 is a mask-ROM member of that family running at about **33–37 MIPS**. A 256-point spectral suppressor would cost about 1–1.5 MIPS, so compute does not rule one out; the shared 6K-word RAM is the tighter limit ([nr_technical_candidates.md](<../research_notes/Noise reduction clock and power/nr_technical_candidates.md>)).

Matsushita also had a fully specified design in the same group's CELP patent family, filed before the device. Morii's spectral-subtraction noise reducer dates from 1994–95 ([US 5,774,846](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5774846); [JP 3183104](https://patents.google.com/patent/JP3183104B2/en)). The Yasunaga / Morii / Ehara family has a November-1996 priority and is the same family the project cites as S-010; its "noise canceller" embodiment specifies a 256-point FFT on 160-sample frames, an 80-sample look-ahead, minimum tracking over 30 frames (0.6 s), over-subtraction ×1.5, a 0.3 reduction coefficient on noise-only frames (−5.2 dB if it applies to power, −10.5 dB if to amplitude; the text read leaves this open), and random phase in the filled bins ([US 6,799,160](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6799160B2/en)). All of it was framed for digital mobile phones, and nothing ties it to a recorder. It is the most plausible in-house template **if** the device did anything, not evidence that it does.

The candidate mechanisms differ in ways a capture can separate, and the codec already produces two of them:

| Candidate | Signature a capture would show | Status in the emulator |
|---|---|---|
| Spectral subtraction (Matsushita / IS-127 style) | the noise level drops 5–20 dB over 0.3–1 s after noise starts; **a sustained tone is attenuated progressively** after about 0.6 s; "watery" residue | not modeled |
| Downward expander / gate on PCM | a static knee in the input/output level curve (slope > 1 below it); "breathing" floor; no adaptation | not modeled |
| VAD + comfort noise or zeroing | pause noise with a frozen spectrum, or digital silence, at frame boundaries | largely redundant with the VAS (A-008, A-022) |
| Decoder postfilter | lower coding noise between formants; no adaptation transient | modeled (A-041) |
| DC/rumble high-pass | more than 1 dB of extra loss at 200–300 Hz beyond the codec template | hidden behind A-027's 225 Hz edge |
| Codec "swirl" on noise | frame-rate-modulated, formant-coloured hiss | present on every unit; its memory is about 4 frames (80–100 ms), so effects on time scales ≥ 0.3 s are not the codec |

Two observations bear on the choice. First, the people who use the device describe the **presence** of hiss ("they both have the hissing sound") and units whose VAS keeps recording in a quiet room at full sensitivity ([ITC Bridge](http://www.itcbridge.com/forum/view_topic.php?id=2062&forum_id=5)). The same reports say other units pause after speech, so this is unit-to-unit spread. It argues mildly against an aggressive suppressor ahead of the VAS. It is also **in tension with the current model** (A-022 × A-048): at level 5 the default model's −62 dBFS hiss sits 16.8 dB (RMS) below the VAS threshold, so it pauses in a silent room. It stops pausing only at a hiss offset of about +9 dB, about −53 dBFS, which is the top of A-048's credible range. The model therefore sits at the "pauses" end of the reported spread. Possible causes of units that keep recording: hotter capsules, a lower threshold, an adaptive detector, or the speaker/earphone path (capture P6). The cheapest test in the whole campaign checks this directly (capture P0 below).

Second, a constraint on the technical note's test plan, sourced in A-008 from S-001: **the RR-DR60 has no VAS switch**. S-001's controls list has no VAS or AGC control, and a 2013 user "really wish[ed] there was a way to disable the voice activation". The note's tests assume "VAS off". On a real unit, steady stimuli must therefore stay above the VAS threshold (about −46 dBFS at level 5, A-022), or the recording must carry a **keep-alive pattern**: a 30 ms, 2 kHz burst at −20 dBFS every 0.7 s, inside A-023's assumed 1.0 s hang. Confirm on the unit first that the pattern keeps it from pausing. Hiss, low-level noise and expander behavior are then read between the bursts. With the pattern, an idle-hiss recording is possible on any unit. Three confounds need care. First, the device's VAS is firmware with an unknown detector, and a noise-floor-tracking VAS would drop a sustained tone, giving a shorter output that an amplitude reading would misread as suppression. So measure output length and splices too (the spec 003 R-11 lesson). Second, VAD plus comfort noise can also turn a steady tone into shaped noise of equal power, so a sustained-tone test must compare the **tonal-to-residual power ratio**, not total power. Third, level 5 may add digital gain (A-033), so stimulus levels should be quoted relative to a limiter-held tone, not as absolute dBFS-equivalents. With those precautions the sustained-tone test is the most discriminating: a minimum-tracking suppressor removes the tone's tonal component progressively, and a gated copy of the same tone passes.

## The MSM7702 datasheet, now read in full, moves three typicals, one limit and one noise figure

S-003's pages 4–17 were the project's longest-standing open lead, and they are now read. The lowercase mirror URL `https://pdf.jiepei.com/msm7702-9605035.html?page=N` serves all 17 pages of E2U0018-28-81 (Aug 1998). Two researchers worked through it, the second from rendered page images. One trap: the conversion prints Symbol-font glyphs as Latin letters, so "±200 mA", "0.1 mF" and "1 ms" on the page are **±200 µA, 0.1 µF and 1 µs** ([msm7702_datasheet.md](<../research_notes/Noise reduction clock and power/msm7702_datasheet.md>); [p. 9](https://pdf.jiepei.com/msm7702-9605035.html?page=9)). The sheet confirms the proxy (S-005, MSM7717) on clocking, the level chain and the template limits. **Full scale is ±0.7 V at GSX, which is +3.0 dBm0**, and the receive side swings ±1.0 V about VDD/2 at AOUT into at least 1.2 kΩ. That confirms A-030's mapping from the MSM7702 itself, and closes A-031's and A-043's open lead on the codec's output level ([p. 8](https://pdf.jiepei.com/msm7702-9605035.html?page=8), [p. 11](https://pdf.jiepei.com/msm7702-9605035.html?page=11), [p. 14](https://pdf.jiepei.com/msm7702-9605035.html?page=14)). The power rows matter most for stage 15. The supply range is 2.7–3.8 V, "Voltage must be fixed". IDD is 5 / 9 mA operating, 0.01 / 0.05 mA in power-down and 1.2 / 3.0 mA in power-save. **PSRR is a single typical figure, 30 dB, over 0–50 kHz with 50 mVpp of ripple**: no minimum and no curve ([p. 12](https://pdf.jiepei.com/msm7702-9605035.html?page=12)). SG is VDD/2 with ±200 µA of drive and 0.1 µF on SGC, and it is "undefined during power saving or power down". Taken literally, the pin descriptions also say AOUT sits "at the voltage level of the signal ground" in power-down, which conflicts as written with SG being undefined ([p. 4](https://pdf.jiepei.com/msm7702-9605035.html?page=4), [p. 7](https://pdf.jiepei.com/msm7702-9605035.html?page=7)). Just as important is what the sheet does **not** give: no PLL lock time, no SG source impedance, noise or settling time, no start-up mute, no internal switched-capacitor clock rate, and no PSRR versus frequency. So A-050's lead "S-003's unread pages" is exhausted without an SG noise figure. A-034's question about the reference's source impedance gets only a drive current. Any record-start or ripple-alias model still rests on bounds, not datasheet values. Every AC figure also stays "not guaranteed" at 6 kHz ([p. 5](https://pdf.jiepei.com/msm7702-9605035.html?page=5)), so A-027's ×0.75 scaling remains an inference that the sheet neither confirms nor contradicts.

## One 8 MHz resonator times both processors, and the codec sits on its 6.0 kHz floor

The clock tree is simpler, and more shared, than the registers say. X1 is a three-terminal **ceramic resonator with built-in load capacitors** (RSXY8M00M07T, "CERAMIC OSC"). It oscillates with one inverter of IC3 (TC7WU04; R37 1 MΩ bias, R69 680 Ω series), and a second inverter buffers it. The buffered 8 MHz feeds **the DSP's OSCF0 (OSCF1 is open), the microcontroller's main clock OSC1, and IC5**. The 32.768 kHz crystal X2 is the microcontroller's sub-clock, not its only clock. IC3 and IC5 sit on the switched "H" rail ([service_manual_power_clock.md](<../research_notes/Noise reduction clock and power/service_manual_power_clock.md>); [S-002](https://archive.org/details/manual_RRDR60_SM_PANASONIC_EN)). The DSP's PLL loop filter is printed: R23 560 Ω, plus R22 56 Ω with C23 0.047 µF to ground, a lag-lead with a pole near 5.5 kHz and a zero near 60 kHz (computed). The phase-detector frequency and VCO gain are unknown, so the loop bandwidth is too. The printed scope annotations are "T≒2us" on BCLOCK, "T≒170us" on RXSYNC2 (which drives both XSYNC and RSYNC) and "F=8MHz 3.2V P-P" at OSCF0. Nothing gives the DSP's core frequency.

The MSM7702 states that XSYNC "triggers the PLL and synchronizes all timing signals". It guarantees its AC characteristics only at 8 kHz ± 50 ppm, and it allows a sync range of **6.0 / 8.0 / 10.0 kHz**, a 1 µs jitter limit and a list of 13 BCLK rates that does not include 500 kHz ([p. 5](https://pdf.jiepei.com/msm7702-9605035.html?page=5), [p. 8](https://pdf.jiepei.com/msm7702-9605035.html?page=8)). The RR-DR60's 6 kHz therefore sits **exactly on the lowest permitted sync**. That has a consequence for the speed control, covered in the next section. It also raises a question about the exact rate. Integer division of 8 MHz cannot give exactly 6000 Hz (8 MHz ÷ 6000 = 1333.3). Exactly 6000 Hz needs a root of 12, 24 or 48 MHz, which means a PLL multiplying by 3 or 6. If BCLK is a plain 8 MHz ÷ 16 = 500 kHz, the nearby integer sync rates are ([clock_codec_rate.md](<../research_notes/Noise reduction clock and power/clock_codec_rate.md>)):

| BCLK | N (BCLK per frame) | fs (Hz) | Period (µs) | vs 6000 Hz | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 500 kHz | 83 | 6024.1 | 166.0 | +0.40 % | the only choice ≥ the 6.0 kHz minimum and within 0.5 % |
| 500 kHz | 84 | 5952.4 | 168.0 | −0.79 % | below the codec minimum |
| 500 kHz | 85 | 5882.4 | 170.0 | −1.96 % | matches the literal "170 µs"; below the minimum |
| 480 kHz (24 MHz ÷ 50) | 80 | 6000.0 | 166.7 | exact | needs a ×3 PLL; "≈ 2 µs" is 2.08 µs |
| 600 kHz (24 MHz ÷ 40) | 100 | 6000.0 | 166.7 | exact | needs a ×3 PLL |

A "≒" scope reading cannot tell 166, 168 and 170 µs apart. A fractional sync averaging exactly 6000 Hz from 500 kHz would carry 1.33 µs of deterministic jitter, over the codec's 1 µs limit, which favours an integer N. All of this is inference. The practical point holds whatever the true value: **any design offset is common to every unit and acts exactly like a per-unit resonator error**. The emulator's nominal 6000 Hz (A-026) stays the right default, and the possible offset belongs as a note on A-011. The same equivalence means **readings of fs from individual units cannot detect a +0.40 % design offset**: it lies inside the ±0.5 % (RSS ±0.6 %) per-unit scatter. Only a resonator-independent ratio can separate them. One is a counter on an opened unit: f_XSYNC / f_8MHz is exactly 1/1328 for the integer design (8 MHz ÷ 16, N = 83) and 1/1333.33 with a ×3 PLL. The other is the coherent spur described below. Failing both, a population mean over at least six units narrows it.

Per-unit scatter is wider than A-011's ±0.5 %, but it matters less than A-011 implies. Murata's CERALOCK figures are ±0.5 % initial, ±0.2 … ±0.5 % over −20 … +80 °C, and ±0.1 … ±0.3 % aging per 10 years ([Murata P17E](https://mm.digikey.com/Volume0/opasdata/d220001/medias/docus/7176/490_p17e.ashx.pdf); [P16E-13](https://media.elv.com/file/03522_keramikschwinger_data.pdf)). The RR-DR60 circuit (an unbuffered inverter and 680 Ω, Murata's standard measuring circuit) adds little to that. For a unit 25–30 years old at room temperature, the computed worst-case sum is **±0.75 … ±0.9 %**, ±0.95 … ±1.4 % across the full temperature band, and the root-sum-square figure is about ±0.6 %. A-011's ±0.5 % is the initial tolerance only. The bigger correction is that a unit records and plays on the same clock, so **a tone recorded at f plays back at exactly f**, whatever the error. What a clock error does scale is everything counted in samples or set by the codec's PLL. The analog stages (microphone corners, limiter RC timings, output chain, speaker) do not move:

| Clock error | Cents (only between record and play) | 2550 Hz band edge moves | 60 min capacity moves |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.1 % | 1.7 | 2.6 Hz | 3.6 s |
| 0.5 % | 8.6 | 12.8 Hz | 18 s |
| 1.0 % | 17.2 | 25.5 Hz | 36 s |

A pitch error needs the clock to **change** between recording and playback. A 25 K temperature swing gives at most 0.09–0.23 % (1.6–3.9 cents), below the 5–6 cent discrimination figure usually quoted ([Wikipedia: Cent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cent_(music)); [Buss et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4192033)). Recordings never leave the unit's own flash, so a per-unit offset never produces a cross-unit pitch error either (inference). Signal-chain row 14's "slight pitch offset between units" therefore describes nothing a listener can hear.

The same reasoning shows that the **field-measurement note is wrong as written**. [field-measurement.md](../../field-measurement.md) says a 1 kHz tone's frequency shift against the phone's crystal "gives the device clock error directly". Through record-then-play on one clock the shift is fs_play/fs_rec − 1, which is about zero on any steady unit. The tone measures the record-to-play clock **change** and the speed setting, not the clock. The same capture can still read the absolute clock in another way: the codec's DAC image. Each decoded component at f has an image at fs − f, and the earphone follower has no low-pass. So **f_tone + f_image = fs**, and a 10 s FFT resolves fs to about 17 ppm. The sum is robust to the coder's own frequency errors, because the image is created in the DAC after the decoder. The spurious row's −35 dBm0 ([p. 12](https://pdf.jiepei.com/msm7702-9605035.html?page=12)) is a worst-case bound for a 3.4 kHz tone with its image at 4.6 kHz. A 1 kHz tone's image at 5 kHz lies deep in the receive low-pass's stopband, plus the hold droop (about −14 dB from droop alone if the D/A holds), so expect it tens of dB below −35 dBm0. The capture should therefore use a **2.2–2.4 kHz tone** (image at 3.6–3.8 kHz), with the earphone volume turned up and the phone's input processing disabled (iOS measurement mode). The alias of a 3.5 kHz record tone, which plays back at fs − 3500, is a second route, with one caveat: a tone about 30 dB down near 2.5 kHz must survive the 4 kbit/s coder. Timing a memory fill reads the ratio between two units. A counter on codec pins 12/13 settles it in minutes on an opened unit. Jitter is not a concern. Even 100 ns RMS would limit a full-scale 2.5 kHz tone to 56 dB SNR, against µ-law's 41–43 dB S/D. Clocks divided from the same 8 MHz alias to DC **only if fs relates to them by an integer**. That holds under N = 83, where 8 MHz = 1328·fs and the leakage lands within about 0.5 Hz of DC. Under an exact 6000 Hz design (8 MHz ÷ 6000 = 1333⅓), the 8 MHz and 500 kHz alias to exactly **2000 Hz**. A faint 2000 Hz line in a silent-input capture would therefore itself identify the design, without opening the unit. The other plausible in-band spurs are incoherent ones: the 32.768 kHz domain (aliases at 2768 and 464 Hz for 6000 Hz) and the boost converter. Neither has been observed, and neither should be modeled without a capture.

## The speed control is firmware, and its slow half cannot be a codec clock change within the datasheet's operating range

The service-manual re-read moves the speed control out of the hardware. **S6 SPEED CONTROL grounds a line with a 1 MΩ pull-up into the microcontroller's IRQ3 (IC4 pin 41)**, not into the DSP. The 8 MHz source is fixed, and the codec's clocks come only from the DSP, so any speed change is a command from IC4 to the DSP's firmware. The likely channel is the P0–P7 host port; the "CHG CLK" → /PMUTE pairing is ambiguous ([service_manual_power_clock.md](<../research_notes/Noise reduction clock and power/service_manual_power_clock.md>)). The manual describes the feature's operation only. There are five levels, factory set to 3, SLOW at 1 and FAST at 5. The level can change only during playback, and its indicator shares the SENS LEVEL bar. Nothing is said about pitch, and there are no ratios ([manual OCR](https://archive.org/stream/Panasonic_RR-DR60_Microcassette_Recorder_User_Manual/Panasonic_RR-DR60_Microcassette_Recorder_User_Manual_djvu.txt); [playback_speed_control.md](<../research_notes/Noise reduction clock and power/playback_speed_control.md>)). The only figure anywhere is the US product page's "**Slow/Fast Play (+/-40%)**" ([Wayback](https://web.archive.org/web/19990913100520/http://www.prodcat.panasonic.com:80/shop/product.asp?sku=RR-DR60)). That page is marketing copy with demonstrable errors ("CLEP", "16 Meg DRAM", "3 AAA"), so the figure should carry low reliability. It is still a primary Panasonic statement, and the speed researcher did not have it.

Put the ±40 % next to the datasheet's 6.0 kHz floor and one mechanism drops out. The conclusion holds for any slow ratio, because normal sits on the floor. "+/-40%" itself has two readings: speed 0.6 / 1.4, or a duration change of ±40 % (slow speed 1/1.4 ≈ 0.714, fast 1/0.6 ≈ 1.67). The table uses the first reading and linear spacing of the five levels, and both are assumptions:

| Level | Ratio (assumed) | Sync needed if the codec clock changes | Within MSM7702 6.0–10.0 kHz? | Content band if pitch shifts (225–2550 Hz stored) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 SLOW | 0.6 | 3.6 kHz | **no** | 135–1530 Hz |
| 2 | 0.8 | 4.8 kHz | **no** | 180–2040 Hz |
| 3 | 1.0 | 6.0 kHz | yes (the floor) | 225–2550 Hz |
| 4 | 1.2 | 7.2 kHz | yes | 270–3060 Hz |
| 5 FAST | 1.4 | 8.4 kHz | yes | 315–3570 Hz |

A clock-based SLOW would push the codec far below its stated operating range, not merely outside the guaranteed point. A designer who planned clock-based slowing would have put "normal" mid-range, not on the floor. Three mechanisms remain. **(a)** A codec clock change for the FAST steps only, a hybrid. **(b)** DSP resampling at a fixed 6 kHz codec rate: pitch-changing. At ratios above 1 it must low-pass the content above 3000/r Hz, or it aliases, so the top of the band is lost. That is a distinguishing artifact: under (a) the whole band moves up intact. **(c)** Time-scale modification in or after the CELP decoder: pitch-preserving. The weak circumstantial lean is toward (c). OKI, the codec maker, held a 1994-priority patent that speeds up or slows down a CELP decoder's output "without altering its pitch" by deleting or repeating whole pitch cycles of the excitation, aimed explicitly at IC-memory answering machines ([EP0714089B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/EP0714089B1/en)). Lucent's excitation-domain method documents the artifacts: "pop and click sounds", "buzzy or noisy" speech, and slowing beyond 1.5× that "often sounds annoying" ([US5717823A](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5717823A/en)). Matsushita had its own semiconductor-memory variable-speed playback patent ([JPH0854895A](https://patents.google.com/patent/JPH0854895A/en)). By 2005 Panasonic marketed its five-step speed control as changing speed "at a natural pitch", from 0.5 to 2× ([ASCII.jp](https://ascii.jp/elem/000/000/346/346061/); [ITmedia](https://www.itmedia.co.jp/lifestyle/articles/0505/30/news062_2.html)). That marketing is seven years later, so it is no direct evidence for this model. No user report found says whether DR60 slow playback sounds deeper or merely slower.

Excitation-domain pitch-cycle repetition (c1) is the cheapest pitch-preserving option on this DSP. It reuses the adaptive-codebook lag already in the bitstream and costs about 10 MAC per output sample (estimated). It quantizes the realized ratio to whole pitch periods per frame and gives voiced speech slight F0 stepping and clicks. On unvoiced segments it gives a metallic, buzzy flutter. SOLA on the decoded output would cost about 1 M MAC/s (estimated) and adds a phasey doubling on transients. None of the three is free in the emulator. Relabelling the playback device rate as 6000·r moves stage 10's receive template to 2550·r Hz, so it builds (a), not (b). The existing output resampler's prototype cutoff is absolute (`CUTOFF_HZ` = 2775 Hz normalized by the prototype rate; `RatePlan::for_host` assumes 6000 Hz). At r = 0.6 the images of 825–1530 Hz content would land at 2070–2775 Hz, inside the passband. **(b) in the emulator is a fixed rational resampler at the 6 kHz device rate, between the decoder and stage 10.** Its ratios are 5/3, 5/4, 1, 5/6 and 5/7 for r = 0.6, 0.8, 1, 1.2 and 1.4. For r > 1 it carries a pre-low-pass at 3000/r Hz. Stage 10 and the output resampler are unchanged, and all five plans are built at construction. **(a)**, if the capture shows it, is the relabelled device rate, with the output resampler's prototype rescaled to 2775·r Hz. (c1) would be new code in the spec 008 decoder, which already carries a pitch lag. Every variant has real-time and API costs. The level changes during playback, so every converter must be built at construction, with no allocation per block. The latency differs per level. Output per block scales by 1/r, which touches `BlockInfo`, the drain and the maximum-output bounds. And the one-pass ≡ record-then-play identity needs a rule, for example "speed applies in the play path only".

One capture separates the mechanisms. Record a steady 1 kHz tone, a **2.3 kHz tone** and a sustained vowel. Then, with S6 ON, play each at levels 1–5 into the earphone return. The level can change only during playback, so each level's window starts after a dial turn. Spectrum to 10 kHz. For 1 kHz at r = 0.6 / 1.0 / 1.4, the three mechanisms predict:

| Mechanism | Tone heard (kHz) | DAC image (kHz) | Σ = tone + image (kHz) | 2.3 kHz tone at levels 4–5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| (a) codec clock change | 0.6 / 1.0 / 1.4 | 3.0 / 5.0 / 7.0 | 3.6 / 6.0 / 8.4, i.e. r·fs | plays at 2.76 / 3.22 kHz |
| (b) DSP resample, fixed clock | 0.6 / 1.0 / 1.4 | 5.4 / 5.0 / 4.6 | 6.0 at every level | **must disappear** (stored content above 3000/r, 2143 Hz at r = 1.4, is removed) |
| (c) time-scale in the decoder | 1.0 at every level | 5.0 at every level | 6.0 at every level | stays at 2.3 kHz |

**The rule: sum the tone and its image at each level, Σ = f_tone + f_image. If Σ tracks the level ratio (r·fs), the codec clock changed: (a). If Σ stays at fs at every level, the codec clock is fixed: (b) or (c), which F0 then separates.** The durations give the five ratios directly. The capture should also note whether the level persists after S6 returns to OFF (a persistent setting or a mode).

## Three switched rails decide what the battery and the REC button can do to the sound

### The boost converter never stops; the firmware switches three loads

The power tree as printed differs from A-013's one-line summary in ways that matter for sound. IC8 is an **XC6372D331PR**: a PWM/PFM auto-switching step-up converter, D type (standby pin plus an **external** switch transistor, here Q8 2SD1119), 3.3 V output, and **100 kHz ±15 %** by Torex's ordering code. Its datasheet gives a slow-start of 4 / 10 / 20 ms and 85 % typical efficiency, but those figures are for the built-in-transistor A types at 5 V output, not the D type fitted here ([Torex XC6371/72](https://uploadcdn.oneyac.com/attachments/files/05/FC/XC6371A501PR-G.pdf); [older family sheet](http://pdf.searchdatasheet.com/b30b978d/torex.co.jp/XC6371B350PL.html)). Its CE pin sits on the R85/R86 270 kΩ divider at Vbat/2 (1.4 V printed), so **it runs whenever batteries are fitted**; no microcontroller pin switches it. From the 3.3 V output the microcontroller switches three things. **P72 (pin 61)** drives Q20, which makes the 3.2 V analog rail (codec, mic amp, limiter, mic-bias filter, earphone follower), and on the same net drives **Q9, which feeds the NJM2113 from the raw battery line**. **P73 (pin 62)** drives Q13/Q10, which make the "H" rail for the flash, the 8 MHz oscillator IC3 and IC5. **P11 (pin 34)** drives the codec PDN and the DSP's /ACFL input. R29 1 MΩ and C30 1000 pF are both shunts to ground on that net, so an undriven port means power-down. P70 drives the amplifier's CD through R91 56 kΩ alone. A rechargeable lithium cell, charged through D6/R81 180 Ω, is switched onto the 3.3 V rail by a Q16–Q18 latch when the batteries come out ("about 3 minutes"). The battery is sensed through R40 100 kΩ into **AN3**, with no divider, so the reading saturates against the 3.3 V reference above about 3.3 V. IC6 is the **XC61AN2002MR** on the 3.3 V rail, driving IC4's /RST. By analogy with Torex's XC61C naming it is a 2.0 V N-channel open-drain detector, so it resets only when the converter collapses; the manual's "batteries have run down when there is actually still some life left" cutoff is firmware ([service_manual_power_clock.md](<../research_notes/Noise reduction clock and power/service_manual_power_clock.md>); [power_supply_effects.md](<../research_notes/Noise reduction clock and power/power_supply_effects.md>)).

The printed voltages describe recording only. The legend reads "No mark ... Recording, ( ) .... Playback", and only 17 points carry a parenthesized value (the host bus and the REC LED port). **Sheet 3.1, which holds all the analog and power circuitry, has none.** If an unmarked value meant "the same in playback", P70 at 3.3 V would hold CD at 2.1 V and mute the speaker during playback, which is impossible. So the convention is "recording; playback only where measured" ([power_sequencing_transients.md](<../research_notes/Noise reduction clock and power/power_sequencing_transients.md>)). The circuit's pull resistors define a safe reset state of "analog off, codec down, clock on". Combined with function and the standby budget, that gives the best current mode table (★ = printed, the rest inferred):

| Rail / enable | Idle stop | Record | Playback, speaker | Playback, earphone |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| IC8 3.3 V boost (CE = Vbat/2) | on | on ★ | on | on |
| H rail: 8 MHz, IC5, flash (Q10, P73) | probably off | on (8 MHz waveform ★) | on | on |
| 3.2 V analog rail (Q20, P72) | **off** (reset default; medium-high) | on ★ | on | on |
| Amplifier supply (Q9, P72) | off | on ★ (2.8 V) | on | on |

The standby argument (an inference) is that Q20/Q9 earn their place only by removing the 0.5–1 mA that PDN and CD cannot: the mic amplifier, the capsule's drain current, the R4 bleed and Q14's bias. On a pair of 1 Ah cells that alone would drain the batteries in about six weeks. What remains open is **when** the firmware raises the rail: at the REC/PLAY press, or on any key or dial activity in a "wake" state. That one choice decides whether a record-start transient exists at all.

### The speaker's clip ceiling tracks the cells; the recording does not

Because Q9 hangs the NJM2113 on the raw battery, the speaker route is the one place battery state reaches the sound. The swing limits (VOH = V+ − 1.1 V, VOL = 0.21 V at 75 mA; [NJM2113](https://www.endrich.com/Datenbl%C3%A4tter/Aktive%20Komponenten/NJM2113_E.pdf)) give a BTL peak ceiling of about **V+ − 1.6 V**. That is A-031's own formula, giving 1.2 V at the sheet's 2.8 V. It is a hard swing limit, whereas the model's 1.15 V is the 10 % THD point, so the table scales the model's ceiling in proportion from the 2.8 V point. The manual's 10 h record / 8 h playback life on alkaline AAA implies about 200 / 230 mW drawn from the cells (≈ 80 / 92 mA at a 2.5 V pair), using Energizer E92 constant-power data ([E92](https://files.batteryjunction.com/frontend/files/energizer-e92bp-24.pdf)). That fixes a discharge trajectory and an implied firmware cutoff near a 2.1–2.2 V pair under load. Taking V+ ≈ pair − 0.2 V (inferred from the 2.8 V mark against an assumed 3.0 V bench supply; Q9's printed values are themselves inconsistent):

| Hours of recording | 0 | 1 | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pair under load (V) | 2.96 | 2.76 | 2.66 | 2.52 | 2.42 | 2.32 | 2.22 |
| Hard swing limit V+ − 1.6 V (V peak diff.) | 1.16 | 0.96 | 0.86 | 0.72 | 0.62 | 0.52 | 0.42 |
| Model ceiling, scaled from 1.15 V at 2.8 V (dB) | −0.3 | −1.9 | −2.9 | −4.4 | −5.7 | −7.3 | **−9.1** |

A-031's "1.0–1.4 V over a pair of AAA cells (3.1 → 2.4 V)" covers only the first one to two hours. By A-031's own formula, 3.1 → 2.4 V gives 1.3 → 0.6 V once the Q9 drop is included. On top of that slow trend the ceiling is **signal-dependent**. A loud passage draws 95–150 mA through 0.8–3.2 Ω of cell and contact resistance, which sags V+ by 0.08–0.48 V, and C20 22 µF smooths only above 2–9 kHz, so a loud syllable lowers its own clip point at speech-envelope rate. That is about −0.9 dB on fresh cells, −2.5 dB at mid-life, and more late in the discharge or in the cold, where cell resistance rises by about 0.4 Ω per cell at 0 °C ([Energizer handbook](https://data.energizer.com/pdfs/alkaline_appman.pdf)). Two simplifications compress or understate these figures. VOH is specified at 75 mA, while 8 Ω draws 50–150 mA, which compresses the span. And Q9's base drive through R26 3.3 kΩ is only 0.39–0.61 mA, a forced beta of 156–390 at 95–150 mA. Q9 is therefore likely out of hard saturation on loud peaks late in the discharge, so "V+ ≈ pair − 0.2 V" understates the dynamic sag. The effect is **audible only on material that reaches the ceiling**: at the reference volume (A-043), −20 dBFS speech keeps about 11 dB of headroom even at 10 h, while limiter-held loud recordings clip. NiMH pairs at 2.4–2.5 V would sit 4–5 dB below fresh alkaline from the start but hold steady. The record path is unaffected because the codec and mic amp run on the regulated 3.3 / 3.2 V rails. The earphone route (Q14 on the 3.2 V rail, amplifier in standby) is immune too.

Supply noise coupling is mostly bounded below audibility, with two low-confidence exceptions ([power_supply_effects.md](<../research_notes/Noise reduction clock and power/power_supply_effects.md>)). In record and playback the estimated 48–59 mA load keeps the converter in fixed 85–115 kHz PWM, at least if L1 is near 100 µH. L1's value is not printed, and with about 47 µH and fresh cells the record load would sit near the PFM boundary, where audio-band pulse skipping becomes possible. The first exception is the boost ripple itself. Through the codec's 30 dB PSRR it is equivalent to **at most −69 dBFS before the rail filter**. That filter is R93 10 Ω with 11.1 µF ahead of the 3.2 V rail the codec sits on: about −37 dB at 100 kHz ideally, realistically about −20 dB with capacitor ESR. The PSRR is also specified only to 50 kHz. So the ripple is likely −90 dBFS or below, and it reaches the band only if the oscillator falls within ±3 kHz of a multiple of the codec's unpublished internal sampling rate. That would give a faint, unit-dependent, drifting tone. The second is the speaker. The NJM2113, with no pin-2 capacitor, rejects battery-line load modulation by only about 27–30 dB, so digital-section current steps could reach the speaker **after** the volume control, as a floor estimated at −70 … −50 dB re the clip level that does not scale with the knob. The mic bias is the best-filtered supply on the board: the Q11 capacitance multiplier has a 0.19 Hz corner, ideally −75 dB at 1 kHz. Neither effect should be modeled without a capture.

### Start-of-item transients hinge on one undocumented firmware choice

If the analog rail and the codec are already up when REC is pressed, there is **no power transient**. A-037's question then reduces to whether the limiter's C6 still holds charge from a recent loud item. If the rail and PDN switch at REC, three things happen ([power_sequencing_transients.md](<../research_notes/Noise reduction clock and power/power_sequencing_transients.md>)). The first is certain given the switching: the **microphone fades in** (R4 39 kΩ × C2 22 µF, τ = 0.858 s). There is nothing from the capsule, not even its hiss, for about 0.2–0.25 s, then reduced sensitivity until about 0.5–0.8 s, roughly −5 dB at 0.4 s for an assumed 0.5 V pinch-off. Firmware cannot blank this without an obvious REC latency. The second is **an SG reference thump** through the 2.09 ms output coupling, bounded at −61 to +4 dBFS in band. It decays below the hiss within 26 ms, and firmware can remove it by discarding the first one or two frames. The third is a **limiter charge**. Only in the worst corner (ΔV_eff ≈ 1.6 V, τ_SG ≤ 2 ms, on the detector's polarity; the 1.6 V / 10 ms case reaches only +1.0 dBFS, 2 dB over the knee, for 6.6 ms, shorter than its 20–25 ms dead time) does the excursion outlast the dead time (A-034). C6 then charges, and the recording starts about 10 dB ducked and recovers over about 1.4 s (A-035). Playback from an idle stop must charge the amplifier's C13, about 0.16–0.35 s (extrapolated from MC34119 behaviour; [MC34119](https://rigpix.com/components/mc34119.pdf)). If the firmware enabled CD before AOUT settled, the AOUT step would reach the speaker as a low-frequency "tock". That would be −3 to −20 dB re full scale for τ_SG of 0.5–10 ms and −34 to −46 dB for 50–200 ms, and it would scale with the volume knob. Firmware that waits about 60 ms after PDN removes it. With an earphone plugged in, a rail power-up would give a **volume-independent thump of about 0.1 V**, of the order of the earphone route's full-volume level (low confidence).

During recording **the speaker is muted by design**. CD sits at 2.1 V, above the 2.0 V standby threshold, with ≥ 70 dB of mute. The margin is thin, though: the divider puts R_CD ≈ 98 kΩ inside the datasheet's 50–175 kΩ, and a part at 50 kΩ would leave CD at 1.56 V, in the undefined band. The earphone path is live. The codec's receive section is clocked (PCMIN averages 0.6 V), and Q14 conducts with a plug in, so whatever the DSP writes reaches the jack at the volume setting. An ITC Bridge user reported hiss "coming out of the speaker during record mode" that the volume control increased. The circuit reading explains that only through the CD-margin caveat combined with a live receive path, or a mislabelled earphone. It is a cheap capture, and it bears directly on the community report that speaker hiss holds the VAS open. For the default model, no speaker-to-microphone feedback path is needed (inference).

## Conclusion

The three "remaining" stages turn out to be one feature, one parameter and one open question. The feature is the speed control: user-facing, documented by Panasonic, and with exactly one undetermined mechanism. The datasheet's 6.0 kHz floor has already narrowed the slow steps to "a DSP resample or a decoder time-scale", with a codec clock change still possible for the fast steps (the hybrid). The parameter is battery state on the speaker route. It is the only power effect the circuit makes plainly audible, and it is already half-written in A-031's formula. The open question is whether the firmware powers the analog rail at REC. Each answer implies a distinct, quantified start-of-item behaviour, and nothing short of a capture chooses between them. Noise reduction and clock deviation, the two items the registers framed as pending models, look on this evidence like records to correct rather than stages to build. The "noise reduction" in the 2020 article most likely describes what the band-pass, the VAS and the postfilter already do (an inference about terminology, not a source). And a clock error can never sound as a pitch error on the unit's own playback.

A second lesson is about the records themselves. About a third of the corrections below come from the service manual's bus letters and printed voltages being read too literally: two A–H brackets on one sheet, and values taken in mixed states. Future schematic-derived rows should cite the pin name and number on both ends of a bus, and should flag any printed value that contradicts the topology.

## Capture tests for a real unit

Ordered by value per minute of effort. P0–P4, P6 and P7 need no opening of the unit (P7 only replaces the cells with a bench supply at the battery terminals). P5 needs the speaker terminals and NJM2113 pin 6, and P8 needs the board. The VAS cannot be disabled, so every acoustic test either keeps steady stimuli above the VAS threshold or carries the keep-alive pattern (a 30 ms, 2 kHz burst at −20 dBFS every 0.7 s, first confirmed on the unit to prevent pausing). Levels are quoted relative to a limiter-held tone, not as absolute dBFS-equivalents, because level 5 may add digital gain (A-033). Output length and splices are recorded alongside levels. **Comparing a unit against the emulator does not cancel the codec**: the emulator's coder is a low-confidence class match (A-040, A-041). Decisions therefore rest on within-unit contrasts that separate by time scale, since the CELP memory is about 4 frames (80–100 ms). The emulator rendering is a sanity reference only. Recommended order for one community campaign, as a single issue-template update: **P0, P1, P3(c), P6, P4**, then the rest.
